Nonwoven Fabrics

Nonwoven fabrics are textile materials made by bonding or interlocking fibers mechanically, thermally, or chemically rather than weaving or knitting them together. They are engineered fabrics widely used in medical, industrial, filtration, and consumer applications.

What Are Nonwoven Fabrics?

Unlike woven fabrics or knitted fabrics, nonwoven fabrics do not rely on yarn interlacing. Instead, fibers are arranged in a web and bonded together. This makes nonwovens highly customizable in thickness, density, strength, absorbency, and softness.

Nonwoven fabrics can be disposable or durable depending on how they are manufactured and what fibers are used.

They can be made from natural fibers such as cotton, wool, or hemp, as well as synthetic fibers like polyester, nylon, and polypropylene.

How Nonwoven Fabrics Are Made

1. Web Formation

Fibers are first laid into a loose web. This can be done by:

  • Dry-laid processes (carding or air-laying)
  • Wet-laid processes (similar to papermaking)
  • Spunbond processes (continuous filament extrusion)

2. Bonding Methods

The fiber web is then bonded using one of several techniques:

Mechanical Bonding

Includes needle punching and hydroentangling (spunlace). Fibers are physically entangled for strength.

Thermal Bonding

Heat is applied to melt or soften fibers so they bond together.

Chemical Bonding

Adhesives or binders are applied to hold fibers together.

Types of Nonwoven Fabrics

Type Manufacturing Method Common Uses
Spunbond Continuous filament extrusion Reusable bags, medical gowns, upholstery backing
Meltblown Fine fiber extrusion with air streams Filters, masks, insulation
Needle-punched Mechanical entanglement Carpets, geotextiles, insulation
Spunlace (Hydroentangled) High-pressure water jets Wipes, medical textiles, hygiene products
Air-laid Air distribution of fibers Absorbent cores (diapers, hygiene)

Key Properties

  • Lightweight or heavy-duty construction
  • High absorbency (if designed for it)
  • Good filtration efficiency
  • Breathability (varies by structure)
  • Low production cost for disposable products
  • Can be engineered for water resistance or barrier protection

Common Applications

Medical & Hygiene

Surgical masks, gowns, wound dressings, diapers, sanitary products.

Filtration

Air filters, oil filters, water purification membranes.

Automotive

Interior insulation, carpeting, trunk liners.

Construction

Roofing membranes, geotextiles, insulation layers.

Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Fast and economical production
  • Highly customizable properties
  • Excellent for disposable and medical uses
  • Can combine multiple functions (filtration + barrier)

Disadvantages

  • Typically lower durability than woven fabrics
  • Limited drape and stretch unless engineered
  • May not be breathable depending on structure
